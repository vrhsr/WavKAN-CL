\begin{abstract}
Beat-level arrhythmia classifiers are often evaluated with beats from one patient in both training and test sets, and their interpretability asserted rather than measured. We present PC-WavKAN, a 153,045-parameter wavelet Kolmogorov--Arnold Network for inter-patient ECG classification. It extends Wav-KAN with physiology-constrained wavelet initialisation (PCWI), which sets each channel group's translation and dilation from QRS, P- and T-wave priors, and trains with a Rarity-Anchored Curriculum (RAC). Evaluation follows the de Chazal DS1/DS2 protocol on MIT-BIH with 20 seeded runs per arm, paired Wilcoxon tests, Holm--Bonferroni correction and Cohen's $d$. The architecture was selected on DS1 validation alone, where the adopted configuration outperformed all seven alternatives tested (Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.0032$); the component ablation identifies PCWI as the only component we introduce with a corrected large effect, and the mother wavelet as interchangeable. That ablation contrasts PCWI only with isotropic initialisation, so whether the benefit is specific to the morphological assignment, rather than to structured multi-scale initialisation generally, remains the study's principal open question. On held-out DS2, Macro-F1 is $0.357\pm0.019$, statistically indistinguishable from four protocol-matched baselines of 29K--118K parameters (all Holm-adjusted $p\geq0.36$; every paired 95\% CI includes zero). RAC yields a large gain in supraventricular recall ($0.137\rightarrow0.222$, $d=+1.47$), though supraventricular precision remains $0.175$. Against an untrained null, PCWI's structure is substantially retained after training: prior retention, not learned alignment. Cross-dataset, PC-WavKAN leads on INCART but trails on SVDB, a bias it shares with B-spline KANs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{C}{ardiovascular} disease is the leading cause of death worldwide, and continuous cardiac rhythm monitoring is central to its management. The electrocardiogram (ECG) remains the reference modality for arrhythmia detection, but manual interpretation of long-term Holter recordings is labour-intensive and does not scale to population screening. Convolutional and attention-based deep networks now report high beat-classification accuracy \cite{takalo-mattila_inter-patient_2018, guo_inter-patient_2019, akan_ecgformer_2024}.

Two properties of that literature limit what those numbers establish. First, much of it evaluates under an \emph{intra-patient} protocol, in which beats from a single recording are distributed randomly across training and test partitions. Because a classifier can then memorise a patient's baseline morphology, accuracies above 97\% are routinely reported that do not transfer to unseen patients \cite{bahrami_investigation_2025, xiao_deep_2023, silva_systematic_2025}. Under the strict inter-patient protocol of de Chazal \emph{et al.} \cite{chazal_automatic_2004}, performance drops sharply, and minority-class recall in particular collapses. Second, transparency claims in this literature are usually qualitative. Models are described as interpretable on the basis of a figure of learned filters, or by attaching a post-hoc attribution method such as Grad-CAM whose output is itself sensitive to noise and to the choice of attribution algorithm \cite{taleban_explainable_2025}.

Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks (KANs) \cite{liu_kan_2024} respond to the second problem: their learnable univariate functions sit on network \emph{edges} rather than at nodes, so the learned function space is inspectable in parameter space rather than only through gradients or occlusion. Bozorgasl and Chen's Wav-KAN \cite{bozorgasl_wavkan_2024} replaces the original B-spline basis with wavelets parameterised by translation and dilation, aligning the basis with classical time--frequency analysis of the ECG \cite{addison_wavelet_2005}. Applications of KANs to ECG such as MAK-Net \cite{zhao_mak-net_2025} show competitive accuracy, but the inspectability of the basis has not been converted into a measured, falsifiable quantity: an inspectable parameter is not the same thing as one that demonstrably encodes physiology.

This work addresses a specific gap at the intersection of those two problems. The wavelet-KAN basis is parameterised in exactly the units --- a temporal/amplitude offset and a width --- in which ECG fiducial morphology is conventionally described. That correspondence can be exploited at initialisation and then \emph{measured} after training. Related work is reviewed in \S\ref{sec:related_work}. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{PC-WavKAN, a physiology-constrained wavelet KAN.} We introduce physiology-constrained wavelet initialisation (PCWI), which partitions the KAN's output channels into QRS-, P- and T-associated groups and initialises each group's wavelet translation and dilation from morphological priors for that component, replacing Wav-KAN's isotropic random initialisation. A component ablation resolved entirely on DS1 validation data identifies PCWI as the only novel component of the architecture with a large, multiplicity-corrected positive effect, and shows that the choice of mother wavelet within the family is not measurably consequential (\S\ref{sec:results_selection}). That ablation is bounded in two ways, both stated wherever the claim is used. It was run against the base configuration from which the final architecture was selected, so PCWI's contribution is established relative to that base rather than re-measured on the adopted model; and it contrasts PCWI with isotropic initialisation, so it shows that structured multi-scale initialisation from these priors helps without showing that the morphological assignment is what makes it help. Closing both is the study's main outstanding experiment (\S\ref{sec:future}).
    \item \textbf{Measured retention of the physiological prior, with an explicit null.} We define Physiological Prior Retention (PPR) as the normalised proximity of the trained wavelet parameters to those priors, and --- unlike interpretability scores reported without a reference point --- we evaluate it against both an untrained PCWI model and a randomly initialised one. This shows what the measurement does and does not license: the trained basis retains physiological structure far beyond a random basis, but the score decreases from its initialisation value, so it quantifies \emph{retention}, not learned alignment (\S\ref{sec:results_ppr}).
    \item \textbf{A curriculum that targets the class the inter-patient protocol breaks.} The Rarity-Anchored Curriculum (RAC) anneals from class-balanced to natural sampling. On the adopted architecture it produces a large, corrected improvement in supraventricular (S) recall and fusion (F) recall at no significant cost to other classes (\S\ref{sec:results_curriculum}).
    \item \textbf{An evaluation protocol strong enough to support a negative result.} All comparisons use 20 seeded runs, paired tests, Holm--Bonferroni correction within declared families, effect sizes and confidence intervals, with the architecture selected without reference to the test set. The honest finding on the field's headline metric is then parity, not superiority (\S\ref{sec:results_primary}).
\end{enumerate}

A model that matches four standard architectures on Macro-F1 while supporting a measurable structural property and a targeted minority-class gain is a different contribution from an accuracy win, and the diagnostic findings this protocol produced are results in their own right.

%% 2. RELATED WORK
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Inter-Patient Evaluation}
Systematic reviews by Xiao \emph{et al.} \cite{xiao_deep_2023} and Silva \emph{et al.} \cite{silva_systematic_2025} document how prevalent intra-patient evaluation remains and how strongly it inflates reported performance; Bahrami \emph{et al.} \cite{bahrami_investigation_2025} quantify the resulting optimism directly. The DS1/DS2 partition of de Chazal \emph{et al.} \cite{chazal_automatic_2004} is the established remedy for MIT-BIH and is the protocol we adopt without modification. Architectures evaluated under it include the CNN--RNN hybrid of Guo \emph{et al.} \cite{guo_inter-patient_2019}, the multiscale attention CNN of Zhou \emph{et al.} \cite{zhou_inter-patient_2024}, and the sequence-to-sequence models of Mousavi \emph{et al.} \cite{mousavi_inter-_2019}. We do not tabulate published accuracies from these works against ours --- differences in lead selection, beat extraction, AAMI mapping and Q-class handling make them non-comparable --- and instead retrain four architectures ourselves under one protocol (\S\ref{sec:baselines}).

\subsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks and Wavelet Bases}
KANs \cite{liu_kan_2024} place learnable univariate functions on edges, with B-splines as the original basis. Wav-KAN \cite{bozorgasl_wavkan_2024} substitutes wavelet functions --- including the Mexican Hat --- parameterised by translation and dilation, arguing that this improves both multi-resolution capacity and interpretability. Our architecture inherits this construction: the wavelet-KAN layer, the Mexican Hat basis, and the argument that a wavelet basis is more inspectable than an opaque activation are due to \cite{bozorgasl_wavkan_2024, liu_kan_2024} and are not claimed here. MAK-Net \cite{zhao_mak-net_2025} pairs spline-activation KAN layers with multiscale convolution and a BiGRU for imbalanced ECG classification, reporting interpretability via Grad-CAM --- post-hoc attribution over inputs rather than a measurement on the basis itself.

What we add to this line of work is not a new basis but a constraint on it and a measurement of that constraint's persistence. Initialisation of KANs is itself an active question: Rigas \emph{et al.} \cite{rigas_initialization_2025} study LeCun- and Glorot-inspired and power-law schemes for KAN basis coefficients, and find the choice materially affects stability and accuracy. That work is deliberately basis- and domain-agnostic and optimises a numerical property --- activation-variance preservation across layers. PCWI is orthogonal to it and complementary: it leaves the coefficient initialisation to such a scheme and instead sets the \emph{translation and dilation} of each channel group from domain priors, which is possible only because those two parameters have units a domain expert can specify. To our reading of this literature, wavelet-KAN translation and dilation have not previously been initialised from ECG fiducial priors, nor has the post-training distance between learned basis parameters and such priors been reported for any KAN-based ECG classifier. We deliberately state this as a gap in reported practice rather than a claim of priority, and we make the accompanying measurement falsifiable by publishing its null baselines (\S\ref{sec:results_ppr}).

\subsection{Class Imbalance and Curricula}
Inter-patient MIT-BIH exhibits N:F ratios above 100:1 \cite{silva_systematic_2025}, under which plain cross-entropy training tends toward majority-class collapse. Curriculum learning \cite{bengio_curriculum_2009} varies the sampling distribution or loss weighting over training. Bae and Shin \cite{bae_handling_2025} show naive contrastive training on imbalanced medical data can \emph{degrade} performance while a curriculum-phased variant recovers it, and Schmale \emph{et al.} \cite{schmale_curriculum_2025} that prioritising rare phenotypes stabilises decision boundaries. Our curriculum is of this family, and \S\ref{sec:results} reports where its benefit is and is not statistically robust. A methodologically distinct alternative operates on logits rather than on sampling: Dai \emph{et al.} \cite{dai_angular_2026} apply adaptive logit adjustment to long-tailed \emph{record}-level multi-label ECG diagnosis, learning bounded label-state-specific prior corrections. That is a different task granularity from the beat-level single-label problem addressed here, so we do not treat it as a comparator, but it is a concrete alternative worth testing at beat level (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Efficient Inference}
Farag \cite{farag_tiny_2023} and Elsheikhy \emph{et al.} \cite{elsheikhy_lightweight_2025} argue for small memory footprints in on-device ECG inference. Rather than infer efficiency from parameter count, we report measured size, x86 CPU latency and post-training quantisation behaviour, and are explicit about the hardware class measured (\S\ref{sec:results_deploy}).

%% 3. METHOD
\section{Method}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Each annotated beat is represented by a signal window $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{T}$ ($T=360$ samples, 360\,Hz, centred on the annotated R-peak) and a five-element inter-beat-interval vector $r_i \in \mathbb{R}^{5}$. The task is to learn $f:(x_i,r_i)\mapsto y_i$ with $y_i \in \{N,S,V,F,Q\}$ under severe imbalance ($N \gg V > S > F \gg Q$).

\subsubsection*{Temporal scope of the task}
Two aspects of this representation make the task \emph{offline beat classification} rather than streaming classification, and we state them here because they bound every claim in this paper. First, $r_i$ contains two intervals that follow beat $i$ (\S\ref{sec:rhythm}), so labelling beat $i$ requires observing beats $i{+}1$ and $i{+}2$. Second, the signal is band-pass filtered over each complete record before beat extraction, which is not a causal operation. A deployment of this model would therefore require a two-beat lookahead buffer and a causal replacement for the record-level filter. \S\ref{sec:results_rr} shows the lookahead is not a formality: the model draws statistically significant signal from one of the two following intervals. We accordingly avoid the terms real-time and streaming throughout.

\subsection{PC-WavKAN Architecture}
The model (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) has a morphology branch and a rhythm branch, fused before classification.

\subsubsection{Wavelet-KAN Morphology Backbone}
Following Wav-KAN \cite{bozorgasl_wavkan_2024}, the learnable function sits on every edge $(j,k)$ rather than at the node (Fig.~\ref{fig:wavkan_micro}): each edge carries an independently parameterised Mexican Hat wavelet $\psi$, the negative normalised second derivative of a Gaussian. The layer maps $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{360}$ to $z_{\mathrm{kan}} \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$,
\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{kan}}^{(k)} = \sum_{j=1}^{360} w_{j,k}\,
\psi\!\left(\frac{x_j - \mu_{j,k}}{\gamma_{j,k}}\right)
+ \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{360} v_{j,k}\, x_j ,
\label{eq:kan}
\end{equation}
for $k=1,\dots,64$, where $\mu_{j,k}$ and $\gamma_{j,k}$ are the learnable translation and dilation of edge $(j,k)$, $w_{j,k}$ its weight, and the second term is a linear residual path with a fixed gate $\lambda=0.1$ that we retain for gradient flow. The output is layer-normalised and passed through a bidirectional GRU (32 hidden units per direction) yielding $z_m \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$. We note explicitly that this recurrent block is applied to a \emph{single-element} sequence: the 64 channel activations form one token, so the recurrence executes one step in each direction from a zero initial state and its output is exactly the concatenation of the two direction states. It therefore performs a gated affine transformation of the channel vector and integrates no temporal context, despite its name. We retain it because every reported result was trained with it and removing it would change the model, but it accounts for 18,816 of the 153,045 parameters ($12.3\%$) and a gated feed-forward block of the same width would be the more honest description of its function. Testing that substitution is left to \S\ref{sec:future}.

\subsubsection{Physiology-Constrained Wavelet Initialisation (PCWI)}
\label{sec:pcwi}
PCWI (\S\ref{sec:pcwi}) is the component that distinguishes this architecture from a generic wavelet KAN. The 64 output channels are partitioned into three groups associated with the ECG components whose morphology the branch must separate, and each group's edge parameters are initialised from a prior for that component rather than from a single isotropic distribution:
\begin{equation}
\mu_{j,k} \sim \mathcal{U}(\bar\mu_c - \epsilon_c,\ \bar\mu_c + \epsilon_c),\quad
\gamma_{j,k} \sim \mathcal{U}(\bar\gamma_c - \delta_c,\ \bar\gamma_c + \delta_c)
\label{eq:pcwi}
\end{equation}
for every edge of the channel group $c$, with $(\bar\mu_c,\bar\gamma_c)$ set from morphology and $(\epsilon_c,\delta_c)$ small jitter terms that break symmetry between edges. PCWI constrains only the translation and dilation; the edge coefficients $w_{j,k}$ retain the implementation's default Kaiming-uniform initialisation, so PCWI is a domain-prior constraint layered on a general-purpose numerical scheme rather than a replacement for one. Purpose-designed KAN coefficient initialisers \cite{rigas_initialization_2025} would substitute for that default without interacting with PCWI, which sets only translation and dilation; we did not evaluate them. Parameters are in units of the per-beat $z$-scored signal (\S\ref{sec:preprocessing}), in which the input is approximately zero-mean and unit-variance. We use channels $1$--$32$ for QRS with $(\bar\mu,\bar\gamma)=(0.0,0.05)$, since the QRS complex crosses the beat's own baseline and is the sharpest transient, so a narrow wavelet is appropriate; channels $33$--$48$ for the P-wave with $(-0.10,0.12)$; and channels $49$--$64$ for the T-wave with $(+0.10,0.10)$, both being slower, lower-amplitude deflections and therefore wider.

We are deliberate about what \eqref{eq:pcwi} is: a physiologically \emph{motivated} allocation of the initial function space, not an assertion that channel group $c$ computes component $c$. The priors constrain only where each group begins, not what it learns, and $\mu$ and $\gamma$ are amplitude-domain quantities corresponding to ECG components through their characteristic amplitude and sharpness rather than by explicit temporal localisation. \S\ref{sec:results_selection} tests whether the allocation helps and \S\ref{sec:results_ppr} tests whether it survives training. Neither test establishes that the \emph{morphological} content of the priors is what helps: the available ablation contrasts \eqref{eq:pcwi} with a single isotropic distribution, so a benefit arising merely from initialising three groups at three different scales would produce the same result. Separating those two explanations requires a control that permutes which group receives which prior while holding the values and the group sizes fixed; we have not run it, and we mark the consequence for the claim in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Gated P-Region Fusion}
Supraventricular beats are distinguished largely by P-wave abnormality (shortened PR interval, inverted or absent P-wave), but the P-wave contributes a small fraction of a beat window's total energy relative to the QRS and T-wave. We therefore add a low-parameter pathway over the pre-R interval: samples $80$--$160$ of the window, i.e.\ $278$\,ms to $56$\,ms before the R-peak, are encoded by a small randomly initialised wavelet-KAN layer, projected to the morphology dimension, and combined with $z_m$ through a learned sigmoid gate that determines per beat how much P-region information enters the main representation. The pathway is implemented with a multi-head attention block, but as it attends over a single key/value token, the softmax is degenerate and the operation reduces to a learned linear projection; the selectivity is supplied by the gate, not by attention weights, and we describe it as gated fusion rather than as attention for that reason. It adds approximately 34K parameters.

\subsubsection{Rhythm Branch}
\label{sec:rhythm}
The rhythm vector is
\begin{equation}
r_i = [\,RR_{-2},\ RR_{-1},\ RR_{0},\ RR_{+1},\ RR_{+2}\,],
\end{equation}
where $RR_{0}$ is the interval from beat $i{-}1$ to beat $i$ (the pre-beat interval of the classified beat), $RR_{-1}$ and $RR_{-2}$ are the two intervals preceding it, and $RR_{+1}$ and $RR_{+2}$ are the two intervals \emph{following} beat $i$. Intervals are in seconds and clipped to $[0.2,3.0]$\,s to bound the effect of missed or spurious detections; no further normalisation is applied. The vector is encoded to $z_r \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$. Two encoders were evaluated: a single-head self-attention block over the five intervals projected to tokens, and a plain multi-layer perceptron over the raw vector. \S\ref{sec:results_selection} selects between them.

\subsubsection{Fusion and Classification}
The concatenation $z = [z_m; z_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{80}$ is classified by an $80\rightarrow48\rightarrow5$ head. With the self-attention rhythm encoder the model has 154,325 trainable parameters; with the plain-MLP encoder selected in \S\ref{sec:results_selection}, 153,045. Both are more than an order of magnitude smaller than MAK-Net (6.1M) \cite{zhao_mak-net_2025} and comparable dense recurrent architectures \cite{guo_inter-patient_2019}. All results outside \S\ref{sec:results_selection} use the 153,045-parameter configuration.

\subsection{Rarity-Anchored Curriculum (RAC)}
\label{sec:rac}
RAC restructures training by class rarity in two phases (Algorithm~\ref{alg:curriculum}). During warm-up (the first 25\% of the epoch budget) mini-batches are drawn by a fully class-balanced sampler and the loss is unweighted cross-entropy, so that no class's decision boundary is established purely by frequency before the rare classes have been seen. During annealing the sampler's balancing exponent $\beta$ decays linearly from 1 (balanced) to 0 (natural), paired with focal loss ($\gamma=2$) under inverse-frequency class weights, with the S-class weight multiplied by 8 to counter its morphological similarity to N. The schedule affects training only; inference always uses the natural distribution. We name it \emph{rarity-anchored} rather than \emph{progressive} to distinguish it from difficulty-ordered curricula: the ordering variable is class frequency, which is known in advance, not per-example difficulty, which is not.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Rarity-Anchored Curriculum (RAC)}
\label{alg:curriculum}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} training set $\mathcal{D}$, epoch budget $E$, warm-up fraction $\alpha=0.25$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} trained model $f_\theta$
\STATE Compute class counts $\{n_c\}$, $c\in\{N,S,V,F,Q\}$; inverse-frequency weights $w_c$; $w_S \gets 8 w_S$
\FOR{epoch $e=1$ to $E$}
\IF{$e \le \alpha E$}
\STATE $\beta \gets 1$; \quad $\mathcal{L} \gets \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ (unweighted)
\ELSE
\STATE $\beta \gets 1 - (e - \alpha E)/(E - \alpha E)$
\STATE $\mathcal{L} \gets \mathrm{Focal}(\gamma{=}2, w_c)$
\ENDIF
\STATE Draw mini-batches with sampling weights $\propto n_c^{-\beta}$
\STATE Update $\theta$ by AdamW; clip gradient norm to 1
\STATE Checkpoint if validation Macro-F1 improved
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Physiological Prior Retention (PPR)}
\label{sec:ppr_def}
To make the effect of PCWI measurable rather than illustrative, we quantify how close the trained wavelet parameters remain to the priors of \eqref{eq:pcwi}. For channel group $c$ with prior $(\bar\mu_c, \bar\gamma_c)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PPR}_c = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{1}{2}\!\left[
\frac{\overline{|\mu_{j,k} - \bar\mu_c|}}{\Delta_\mu}
+ \frac{\overline{|\,|\gamma_{j,k}| - \bar\gamma_c|}}{\Delta_\gamma}
\right]\right),
\label{eq:ppr}
\end{equation}
where the overlines denote a mean of absolute per-edge deviations over all edges of group $c$, and $\Delta_\mu = 0.40$, $\Delta_\gamma = 0.20$ are fixed normalising spans chosen once as the plausible parameter ranges over all three components. The reported score is the unweighted mean of $\mathrm{PPR}_c$ over the three groups.

Two properties of \eqref{eq:ppr} must be stated for the resulting number to be interpretable, and both were established empirically rather than assumed (\S\ref{sec:results_ppr}). First, PPR is a \emph{retention} measure: since PCWI initialises at the prior, an untrained PCWI model scores near-maximally by construction, so PPR can only decrease during training and a high value cannot show that the model \emph{learned} physiological structure. Interpreting it requires the untrained value as a reference. Second, \eqref{eq:ppr} averages each individual edge's absolute deviation. Taking instead the deviation of each group's \emph{mean} parameter --- the more obvious construction --- is degenerate, because per-edge drift is close to symmetric and largely cancels: we measure a mean per-edge $|\Delta\mu|$ of $0.064$ against a group-mean $|\Delta\mu|$ of $0.002$, a factor of about 30. PPR is defined only for a PCWI-parameterised wavelet layer; it is not a general interpretability metric and does not apply to the comparators.

\section{Data and Evaluation Protocol}

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
MIT-BIH \cite{moody_impact_2001, goldberger_physiobank_2000} lead II is band-pass filtered per record, and a 360-sample window is extracted around each annotated R-peak. Each beat is $z$-scored using \emph{its own} mean and standard deviation; no normalisation statistic is estimated across beats, records or splits, so no distributional information can cross the split boundary. Beats with degenerate variance are discarded. Inter-beat intervals are computed from annotation sample indices as described in \S\ref{sec:rhythm}.

\subsection{Inter-Patient Split}
We use the DS1/DS2 partition of de Chazal \emph{et al.} \cite{chazal_automatic_2004}, splitting DS1 further into training and validation records following Takalo-Mattila \emph{et al.} \cite{takalo-mattila_inter-patient_2018}. DS1 training uses the 18 records 101, 106, 108, 109, 112, 114, 115, 116, 118, 119, 122, 124, 201, 203, 205, 207, 215 and 220; DS1 validation the 4 records 208, 209, 223 and 230, which carry every model and architecture selection decision; and DS2, held out, the 22 records 100, 103, 105, 111, 113, 117, 121, 123, 200, 202, 210, 212, 213, 214, 219, 221, 222, 228, 231, 232, 233 and 234. The partition is by record, so no patient contributes beats to more than one of the three subsets; disjointness is asserted programmatically and covered by a regression test. Checkpoint selection, early stopping and all architecture and hyperparameter decisions use DS1 validation only. DS2 is used to report the results of \S\ref{sec:results_primary} onward.

\subsection{Class Distribution and AAMI Mapping}
Annotations are mapped to the five AAMI EC57 super-classes \cite{xiao_deep_2023, luz_survey_2016} (Table~\ref{tab:class_dist}). No class is excluded from the loss: every class receives the same inverse-frequency weight, with the single exception of the S-class multiplier of \S\ref{sec:rac}. \subsection{External Datasets}
\label{sec:external_data}
Cross-dataset evaluation uses the St Petersburg INCART 12-lead Arrhythmia Database \cite{goldberger_physiobank_2000} and the MIT-BIH Supraventricular Arrhythmia Database (SVDB) \cite{greenwald_improved_1990}. Both are processed by the identical pipeline described in \S\ref{sec:preprocessing} --- same window length, same per-beat $z$-scoring, same interval formula, same clipping, same AAMI mapping --- and both carry genuine expert per-beat annotations, so they are label-compatible with MIT-BIH at the granularity the model is trained on. SVDB was assembled specifically to enrich supraventricular ectopy and therefore functions as an S-class stress test. Neither dataset was used for any training, tuning or selection decision; both are evaluated forward-pass only from MIT-BIH-trained checkpoints.

We also processed PTB-XL \cite{wagner_ptb-xl_2020} but \textbf{do not report it as a generalisation result}, because our extraction is not distribution-compatible with the model's training inputs in three independent ways, each sufficient to confound the measurement. (i) The beats are not $z$-scored, so the model receives raw millivolts where \eqref{eq:pcwi}'s priors and the trained dilations $\gamma\approx0.05$--$0.12$ are defined on the standardised scale; a narrow wavelet evaluated far from its translation contributes almost nothing, leaving the morphology branch largely inactive rather than merely shifted. (ii) The interval features are divided by a local 10-beat running mean, giving dimensionless ratios near unity instead of the seconds the rhythm branch was trained on, which also compresses the prematurity contrast it depends on. (iii) PTB-XL's SCP labels are record-level and our extraction applies one label per beat, so beat-level recall against them does not measure beat-level correctness. A Macro-F1 of $0.200\pm0.005$ over 20 seeds was obtained; it reflects preprocessing artefact rather than transfer and we exclude it from all claims, stating it here so that the absence of a widely used benchmark is accounted for. Repeating the extraction under \S\ref{sec:preprocessing} is future work.

\subsection{Baselines and What Is Matched}
\label{sec:baselines}
Four architectures spanning 29K--118K parameters are compared (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). Because the phrase ``identical protocol'' is often used loosely, we state exactly what is and is not held constant.

\emph{Held identical:} dataset, preprocessing, DS1/DS2 split, input representation (both the beat window and the interval vector, so no model sees features another does not), optimiser (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine-annealed schedule, epoch budget (100), early-stopping patience (15), batch size (64), gradient-norm clipping (1.0), the checkpoint-selection rule (best DS1-validation Macro-F1), the set of 20 random seeds, and the class-weight formula.

\emph{Not held identical:} the imbalance-handling mechanism, deliberately, since RAC is a contribution under test rather than a nuisance variable. The baselines use natural-distribution sampling with class-weighted cross-entropy, except CNN+Focal which uses focal loss without static class weights, matching each method's own standard formulation. We tested one uniform imbalance mechanism across all five models and rejected it: stacking a class-balanced sampler with inverse-frequency weighting at this imbalance ratio drives the majority class's effective gradient to near zero, and three of the four baselines then failed to predict class N at all. Degrading baselines with a mechanism chosen to suit the proposed model is not a fair comparison. PC-WavKAN additionally applies minority-class signal augmentation; \S\ref{sec:results_augmentation} shows this does not account for its result, since removing it leaves Macro-F1 statistically unchanged.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison} should therefore be read as comparing complete methods --- architecture together with its imbalance strategy --- under a common protocol and budget, not as isolating the architecture alone; the within-family ablation of \S\ref{sec:results_selection} is what isolates architectural components.

\subsection{Statistical Methodology}
\label{sec:stats}
Every arm is trained with the same 20 random seeds --- 42, 101, 777, 2026, 9999, 1234, 2024, 31415, 27182, 7, 11, 13, 888, 555, 333, 99, 1001, 5050, 8080 and 1998 --- and every comparison is paired by seed: seed $s$ of one arm is compared with seed $s$ of the other, so the pairing corresponds to a shared initialisation and data-order stream. Twenty seeds per arm keeps the study's roughly 400 training runs tractable. We state the resulting sensitivity rather than assert its adequacy: at $n=20$ and $\alpha=0.05$ two-sided, a paired test attains $80\%$ power only for a standardised paired effect $d_z \gtrsim 0.66$, and has approximately $57\%$ power against Cohen's medium $d_z=0.50$; the Wilcoxon test is marginally less efficient still. The study is therefore powered for large effects, and we report non-significant results with confidence intervals rather than as evidence of absence. The count was fixed before any comparison was run, and no arm was later extended or truncated.

Significance is assessed by the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test \cite{wilcoxon_individual_1945}, which does not assume normality of the per-seed differences. Because the study reports many metrics, we declare comparison families explicitly and apply Holm--Bonferroni correction \cite{holm_simple_1979} within each: the metrics of a single arm-vs-arm comparison form one family (five metrics for a per-class comparison, four where fusion recall is not estimable), and the set of comparisons against the four baselines on one metric forms one family. All $p$-values reported are Holm-adjusted within the stated family unless labelled raw. Effect sizes are Cohen's $d$ \cite{cohen_statistical_1988} for paired differences, $d = \bar{\delta}/s_\delta$, and we adopt one sign convention throughout: \textbf{positive $d$ favours PC-WavKAN, the adopted configuration, or the treatment arm}. We additionally report 95\% confidence intervals on the paired mean difference, because for the primary comparison the width of that interval is more informative than the non-significance of the test.

We distinguish three levels of claim. The \emph{primary} outcome is DS2 Macro-F1 for the adopted configuration against the four baselines (\S\ref{sec:results_primary}); \emph{secondary}, pre-specified outcomes are the per-class recalls on which the curriculum claim rests (\S\ref{sec:results_curriculum}); everything else --- the rhythm-position ablation, the cross-dataset analysis, the few-shot curves and the augmentation comparison --- is \emph{exploratory}, corrected within its own family where a test is reported and not to be read as confirmatory.

Three findings follow, and they revise the account of the architecture its initial design implied.

\emph{PCWI is the architecture's effective novel component.} Replacing \eqref{eq:pcwi} with isotropic random initialisation costs $0.021$ validation Macro-F1 with a large effect ($d=-1.05$, Holm $p=1\times10^{-4}$). This is the only positive contribution among the components we introduced that survives correction. The comparison is against isotropic initialisation, so it licenses the claim that initialising the basis in three physiologically-derived groups helps, and not the stronger claim that the specific morphology-to-group assignment is responsible: three arbitrary distinct scales might do as well. We did not run the permuted control that would separate these (\S\ref{sec:limitations}, item 9). Because every row of Table~\ref{tab:family_ablation} varies one component of the \emph{base} configuration, this establishes PCWI's contribution within that base; the adopted configuration differs from it only in the rhythm encoder, and the two components act on different branches of the network, so we expect the effect to carry over --- but we have not measured it there, and we do not present it as though we had. Re-running this single ablation on the adopted configuration is a well-defined experiment requiring no new methodology, and is the study's main outstanding item (\S\ref{sec:future}).

\emph{The mother wavelet is interchangeable.} Substituting Morlet, DOG or a B-spline for the Mexican Hat changes validation Macro-F1 by at most $0.010$, and none of the three differences approaches significance; Morlet is in fact numerically higher than the base. The Mexican Hat is a reasonable choice on the standard argument that its shape matches a sharp biphasic transient \cite{addison_wavelet_2005}, but our data do not support the claim that this choice is what makes the model work. What matters measurably is \emph{where} the basis is initialised, not \emph{which} basis it is.

\emph{The self-attention rhythm encoder is harmful and is removed.} Replacing it with a plain MLP over the same five intervals \emph{improves} validation Macro-F1 by $0.025$ with a large effect ($d=+1.15$, Holm $p=8\times10^{-4}$), and is the best of all seven configurations on validation. We adopt this 153,045-parameter configuration. \S\ref{sec:discussion_rr} discusses why self-attention over five scalars is a poor inductive bias here.

\subsubsection*{Disclosure regarding test-set exposure}
Table~\ref{tab:family_ablation} is a validation-only analysis and its ranking is sufficient to select the adopted configuration without reference to DS2. We note for completeness that DS2 metrics for the non-adopted variants were also computed during development, and that the DS2 ranking agrees with the validation ranking (the adopted configuration is also highest on DS2, at $0.357\pm0.019$ against $0.323\pm0.018$ for the base). DS2 was therefore not literally untouched over the whole course of this study, and we do not describe it as such. The design does guarantee that the selection rule is fully determined by validation data, that no hyperparameter, threshold or checkpoint was chosen on DS2, and that \S\ref{sec:results_primary} compares one pre-selected configuration against four baselines under the same rule. Conservatively, \S\ref{sec:results_primary} should be read as a held-out estimate under a validation-determined selection procedure rather than a single-shot test result; its conclusion is a null result, which selection bias does not manufacture.

\subsubsection*{Comparison with published inter-patient results}
We stated in \S\ref{sec:related_work} that we do not tabulate published
accuracies against ours, because lead selection, beat extraction, AAMI mapping
and Q-class handling differ between studies. That position should not be used
to avoid an unfavourable comparison, so we state the most direct one
explicitly. de Chazal \emph{et al.} \cite{chazal_automatic_2004}, whose
partition we adopt unmodified, report SVEB sensitivity $0.759$ at a positive
predictivity of $0.385$, and VEB sensitivity $0.777$ at a positive
predictivity of $0.819$. On the S class their linear-discriminant system is
therefore substantially better than every deep model trained here on
\emph{both} axes, and no difference in operating point accounts for it; on the
V class our recall of $0.898$ exceeds theirs while our precision of $0.528$ is
lower. Their system uses two leads, hand-engineered morphology sampled at
fiducial points, and heartbeat-interval features computed against a
record-level average, where ours uses a single lead and a fixed window. We do
not claim these numbers are interchangeable, and we have not rerun their
method. We report the comparison because the S-class gap is large, is against
the protocol's own source, and is not explained by anything in this study ---
it is the clearest evidence that beat-level supraventricular detection under
the inter-patient protocol is not yet solved by architectural work of the kind
reported here.

Masking $RR_{0}$, the interval immediately preceding the classified beat, causes by far the largest degradation. This is the expected result and we present it as a sanity check rather than a discovery: a shortened pre-beat interval is the textbook signature of a premature ectopic beat, so it should be the single most important element of the vector, and the fact that the model's dependence structure recovers this is evidence that the rhythm branch is using the feature the way clinical reasoning would.

The consequential row is $RR_{+2}$. Its effect is small but survives correction, which means the model draws real diagnostic signal from an interval that has not yet elapsed when beat $i$ would need to be labelled in a streaming deployment. Together with the record-level filtering noted in \S\ref{sec:methods}, this establishes the non-causality of \S\ref{sec:methods} as a substantive property rather than a formality: simply zeroing the two post-beat positions at inference would be expected to cost accuracy, not merely to remove an unused input. We report the corresponding V-recall change at $RR_0$ ($-0.0745\pm0.1132$) as a raw effect only, since we did not include V-recall in this analysis's test family.

Training moves the wavelet parameters substantially: PPR falls from its near-maximal initial $0.973$ to $0.783$, and the mean absolute per-edge translation deviation grows from the $0.013$ contributed by PCWI's symmetry-breaking jitter to $0.064$, a factor of five on a normalising span of $0.40$. The QRS group retains the most structure ($\mathrm{PPR}=0.814$), the P and T groups slightly less ($0.764$, $0.770$). This ordering is by absolute parameter deviation, not by visual similarity of the resulting functions: in Fig.~\ref{fig:wavelets} the QRS curves look more changed than the P curves because a wavelet initialised narrow ($\bar\gamma=0.05$) changes shape more conspicuously than a wide one ($\bar\gamma=0.12$) for the same absolute change in $\gamma$.

So the defensible claim is that the physiologically structured function space established by \eqref{eq:pcwi} is \emph{retained}, not discarded, under gradient descent, while still being refined by it --- and \S\ref{sec:results_selection} independently shows this initialisation is the component that measurably improves validation performance. The two findings together support a specific and modest interpretation: constraining where the basis starts is useful, and the useful structure persists. They do not support the stronger claim that the model learned physiological alignment, which the null in Table~\ref{tab:ppr} directly refutes. Nor is PPR a measure of interpretability in the sense of explaining a prediction: it is computed entirely from parameters and never observes a model output. We report it as a transparency property of the parameterisation, and we regard publishing its null as inseparable from reporting the score at all. This follows established practice rather than introducing it: Heap \emph{et al.} \cite{heap_automated_2025} show that automated interpretability metrics can score randomly initialised transformers as highly as trained ones, and argue for routine randomised baselines on exactly these grounds. Our contribution here is to supply such baselines for a parameter-space measure on a KAN, not to propose the practice. Fig.~\ref{fig:wavelets} shows the trained bases against their priors.

The pattern is dataset-dependent and we do not summarise it as a single verdict. On INCART, PC-WavKAN has the highest Macro-F1 of the five models and is significantly better than three of the four baselines with large effects, but it is \emph{not} significantly better than B-Spline KAN ($d=+0.45$, Holm $p=0.083$); the honest reading is that the two KAN models lead this dataset and the numerical ordering between them is unresolved. On SVDB, PC-WavKAN is significantly worse than the same three baselines, again with large effects, and again statistically tied with B-Spline KAN.

That the two KAN models track each other on both datasets --- leading together on one, trailing together on the other --- points to shared KAN structure rather than anything specific to PC-WavKAN. A per-class analysis over summed confusion matrices localises the mechanism. On SVDB both KAN models misassign supraventricular beats to V at a nearly identical, elevated rate (PC-WavKAN $0.408$, B-Spline KAN $0.412$) markedly above all three non-KAN baselines (ResNet1D $0.173$, CNN+Focal $0.118$, Transformer $0.319$). Both also over-predict V there ($17.8\%$ and $18.9\%$ of all predictions, against $8.3\%$ and $5.5\%$ for ResNet1D and CNN+Focal) and correspondingly attain the two highest V-recalls of the five models. The bias that helps ventricular detection is what costs supraventricular detection, and on a dataset enriched for supraventricular ectopy that trade is unfavourable. The same signature is far weaker on INCART, where PC-WavKAN's S$\rightarrow$V rate ($0.280$) exceeds the highest non-KAN baseline ($0.253$) by only $0.027$, against a gap of $0.089$ on SVDB. This localises the failure mode --- S$\rightarrow$V confusion tied to V over-prediction, shared by both KANs and strongest where supraventricular beats are most prevalent --- without explaining why the KAN parameterisation induces it (\S\ref{sec:future}).

\subsection{Few-Shot Domain Adaptation}
\label{sec:results_fewshot}
As an exploratory analysis we fine-tuned each zero-shot checkpoint on $k \in \{0,10,50,100,500\}$ beats per class from a record-disjoint adaptation pool, fixed by a constant split seed before any model saw the data and held out from evaluation. Adaptation reallocates recall between classes rather than fixing transfer, and whether that helps the aggregate is dataset-dependent. On SVDB it works clearly: at $k=500$ S-recall rises almost fivefold to $0.506$ ($d=+7.07$) and Macro-F1 from $0.271$ to $0.331$ ($d=+4.58$), even as V-recall falls. On INCART Macro-F1 is unchanged ($0.392\rightarrow0.396$), because a V-recall gain to $0.850$ ($d=+2.96$) is cancelled by S-recall halving from $0.571$ to $0.295$ ($d=-1.90$); that curve is non-monotonic, peaking at $k=50$ ($0.643\pm0.095$) before falling below its own zero-shot value. Choosing $k$ therefore requires the whole curve and the class of interest, not a single favourable operating point. Full curves and the per-seed values are in the released results.

\subsection{Augmentation}
\label{sec:results_augmentation}
PC-WavKAN's recipe applies minority-class signal augmentation while the baselines do not (\S\ref{sec:baselines}), so we quantify what that asymmetry is worth. Under an isolated recipe (60 epochs, no RAC, 20 seeds) in which augmentation is the only active mechanism, all three noise-based strategies significantly raise V-recall (Holm $p<0.002$, $d\geq+1.16$) and significantly \emph{reduce} Macro-F1 (Holm $p\leq0.033$, $d\in[-1.08,-0.50]$), none reaching significance on S-recall. SMOTE is the clearest trade, giving the largest S-recall gain in this study ($0.316\rightarrow0.648$, $d=+3.01$) at the largest Macro-F1 cost ($d=-4.58$); we do not recommend it, consistent with Bae and Shin \cite{bae_handling_2025}.

That Macro-F1 cost does \emph{not} carry over to the full recipe, which is what matters for interpreting Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison}. Under the actual training configuration (100 epochs, RAC, 20 seeds), removing augmentation leaves Macro-F1 statistically unchanged ($0.357\rightarrow0.364$, Holm $p=0.46$, $d=+0.25$) while significantly reducing F-recall ($0.0061\rightarrow0.0022$, Holm $p=0.043$) and trending toward lower V-recall ($d=-0.70$, Holm $p=0.083$). So the augmentation asymmetry does not explain PC-WavKAN's parity result; and a penalty visible in a short single-mechanism ablation was masked once RAC and the full budget were present, which is a caution against extrapolating such ablations to full recipes.

Post-training dynamic INT8 quantisation gives essentially nothing here and makes inference slower. This is explicable rather than anomalous: PyTorch's dynamic quantisation converts \texttt{nn.Linear} modules only, whereas this model's parameters sit almost entirely in the custom wavelet-KAN layer and the bidirectional GRU, so the pass adds dequantisation overhead without accelerating the dominant part of the graph. An efficiency claim for a KAN-based architecture should therefore rest on measured FP32 latency and parameter count, not an assumed quantisation benefit; realising one would require a quantised wavelet-layer implementation.

At $2.93$ ms per beat single-threaded and $0.429$ ms batched, throughput far exceeds the 1--2 beats per second of a resting adult, so the model is compatible with continuous processing on a general-purpose CPU. This is not evidence of wearable deployability: these are x86 measurements, and we have not benchmarked the ARM Cortex-class hardware on-device Holter analysis would use, where the absent quantisation path matters more. We therefore claim resource efficiency with measured x86 CPU inference and no more, and do not tabulate against MAK-Net's 24 ms/beat GPU figure \cite{zhao_mak-net_2025}, whose hardware class differs.

%% DISCUSSION
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What This Study Establishes}
Three findings are supported at the level of rigour the protocol was built for. Physiologically derived initialisation of a wavelet-KAN basis measurably improves validation performance within the configuration it was ablated in, is the only component we introduced with a corrected large effect there, and its structure is substantially retained through training. A rarity-anchored curriculum produces a large, reproducible improvement in supraventricular recall at no robust cost elsewhere, replicated across two architectural variants, two recipes and both partitions. And under a matched protocol with multiplicity control, the architecture is statistically indistinguishable from four standard baselines on Macro-F1, with every paired confidence interval containing zero.

The third constrains how the first two should be read. PC-WavKAN is not more accurate than a 30K-parameter Transformer here, and we do not present the wavelet-KAN basis as an accuracy mechanism. What it offers is a parameterisation whose learned function space is expressible in morphological terms, can be initialised from physiological knowledge and measured against it afterwards --- obtained, on this evidence, at no accuracy cost. Whether that is worth the parameter budget depends on how much an application values inspectability of the basis, which is an argument about design priorities and separate from the accuracy evidence. On protocol, the study satisfies all four criteria of Silva \emph{et al.}'s E3C framework \cite{silva_systematic_2025}, independently of the parity finding.

The ablation also narrows our own architectural claim: the mother wavelet does not matter measurably (Table~\ref{tab:family_ablation}), so the appeal to the Mexican Hat's suitability for sharp QRS transients \cite{addison_wavelet_2005}, which motivated the design, is not what the data support. The transferable insight concerns \emph{initialisation} in a parameterisation whose parameters have physiological units, and should apply to any wavelet basis.

\subsection{Why Self-Attention Over Interval Features Fails}
\label{sec:discussion_rr}
Removing self-attention from the rhythm encoder improved validation Macro-F1 with a large effect (Table~\ref{tab:family_ablation}), and this is not in tension with \S\ref{sec:results_rr}'s finding that the interval features carry real signal. The two results together indicate that the problem is the encoder, not the features.

Self-attention computes content-based pairwise interactions over a set and is close to permutation-equivariant absent positional information --- well matched to long sequences whose structure is relational. The interval vector is the opposite case: five elements whose information is sharply concentrated in one \emph{position} ($RR_0$, \S\ref{sec:results_rr}), whose diagnostic meaning derives entirely from the position it occupies. An encoder biased toward order-insensitive relational mixing must spend capacity relearning a distinction a plain MLP over a fixed-length vector has for free. The negative result is therefore informative: attention appears to be the wrong primitive for short fixed-length physiological feature vectors with strong positional semantics, and this should be checked rather than assumed.

\subsection{The Cross-Dataset Pattern}
The informative structure in \S\ref{sec:results_crossdata} is that the two KAN models move together: both lead on INCART, both trail on SVDB, and both share the S$\rightarrow$V confusion the non-KAN baselines do not. Since that signature spans a wavelet and a B-spline basis, it is unlikely to be specific to the wavelet parameterisation. The more plausible reading is that the KAN edge parameterisation, transforming each input sample by its own univariate function before summation, favours localised amplitude-morphology evidence over the broader context separating supraventricular from ventricular ectopy when the two are morphologically close, as in SVDB's population. This is a hypothesis consistent with the diagnostic, not a demonstrated mechanism, and is testable by ablating the layer's locality with the rest of the architecture fixed. The result also cautions against reporting a single external dataset: on INCART alone PC-WavKAN would appear the best of five at transfer, on SVDB alone among the worst, and either conclusion would carry 20 seeds and corrected statistics.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\noindent\textbf{(1) Parity, not advantage.} Macro-F1 is statistically indistinguishable from four baselines (\S\ref{sec:results_primary}); a reader expecting an accuracy gain from a new architecture will not find one. The intervals bound any true difference at roughly $\pm0.03$ but do not establish equivalence, for which we set no margin.

\noindent\textbf{(2) Absolute minority-class performance.} F-recall is negligible throughout, spanning $0.0003$ to $0.0061$ across the configurations reported here, reflecting 388 test beats and 399 training beats; the significant relative improvement in Table~\ref{tab:curriculum_stats} has no practical clinical reading at that absolute level. Q, with 15 beats in the database, supports no per-class conclusion at all and inflates the variance of any five-class macro average. S-recall is $0.198$ for the adopted configuration (Table~\ref{tab:perclass}); the $0.222$ of Table~\ref{tab:curriculum_stats} belongs to the augmentation-free arm used to isolate the curriculum. Either way roughly four in five supraventricular beats are missed, at a precision of $0.175$.

\noindent\textbf{(3) Selection history of the test set.} The adopted configuration is fully determined by validation data (\S\ref{sec:results_selection}), but DS2 metrics for non-adopted variants were computed during development, so DS2 is not a never-inspected partition and we do not describe it as one.

\noindent\textbf{(4) Non-causal inputs.} The interval vector includes two post-beat intervals and the filter is applied per record (\S\ref{sec:methods}), so the model performs offline classification and would need a two-beat lookahead buffer and a causal filter to run in a streaming setting. \S\ref{sec:results_rr} shows the lookahead carries significant signal, so the cost of removing it is real and, as yet, unquantified.

\noindent\textbf{(5) External-data scope.} PC-WavKAN is significantly below three of four baselines on SVDB (\S\ref{sec:results_crossdata}); the failure mode is localised to an S$\rightarrow$V bias shared with B-Spline KAN, but its cause in the KAN parameterisation is a hypothesis, not a result. Our PTB-XL extraction is separately confounded by three preprocessing and labelling mismatches (\S\ref{sec:external_data}), so cross-dataset evidence rests on two databases only, both sharing MIT-BIH's annotation granularity and processed identically; generalisation to 12-lead clinical recordings with record-level labels is untested here.

\noindent\textbf{(6) Adaptation trades classes, and hardware scope is narrow.} Few-shot adaptation improves the classes it is given data for at other classes' expense, non-monotonically in $k$ on INCART (\S\ref{sec:results_fewshot}); we have not characterised which target distributions make the trade favourable. Separately, latency is x86 CPU only --- no ARM-class or on-device measurement was made --- and dynamic INT8 quantisation does not benefit this architecture (\S\ref{sec:results_deploy}).

\noindent\textbf{(7) The PCWI evidence is bounded in two ways, and the second bounds the word ``physiology''.} First, every row of Table~\ref{tab:family_ablation} varies one component of the \emph{base} configuration, which is the correct design for the selection that table performs, but it means PCWI has been ablated there and not on the model we adopt. The two differ solely in the rhythm encoder and PCWI acts on the morphology branch, so we expect the effect to transfer; that is reasoning, not measurement. Second, and more consequentially, the ablation contrasts PCWI with a single isotropic distribution. A benefit that arose purely from initialising three channel groups at three different scales --- with no morphological content at all --- would produce exactly this result. Our evidence therefore supports \emph{structured, physiologically-derived multi-scale initialisation helps}, and does not support \emph{the morphological assignment is what makes it help}, which is the reading the method's name invites. The decisive control is to permute which group receives which prior while holding the prior values, the group sizes and every training detail fixed, so that only the morphology-to-group mapping varies; the implementation is included in the released code but the experiment has not been run. Until it is, the physiological interpretation of PCWI should be read as a design rationale rather than a measured finding. The same base-configuration caveat applies to the P-region pathway and the basis comparison, whose effects were in any case unresolved after correction.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
Two experiments on the adopted configuration would settle the central architectural claim, each 20 seeds with no new methodology (\S\ref{sec:limitations}, item 9). The first re-runs the PCWI ablation there, converting a claim established on the base configuration into one measured on the model proposed. The second, and the more important of the two, is the permuted-prior control: the same three channel groups and the same prior values, with the morphology-to-group assignment permuted, so that a difference can only be attributed to the assignment itself. If the physiological assignment does not beat a permuted one, the contribution is structured multi-scale initialisation rather than physiological initialisation, and the method should be renamed accordingly; we regard that outcome as entirely possible on the present evidence. A further substitution worth testing is replacing the single-step recurrent block (\S\ref{sec:methods}) with a gated feed-forward layer of equal width, which would recover $12\%$ of the parameter budget if performance is unchanged. Second is to repeat the PTB-XL evaluation under the pipeline of \S\ref{sec:preprocessing} and against beat-level annotations, which would convert an excluded, confounded result into a real test of transfer to 12-lead clinical recordings. Beyond those we prioritise: identifying the mechanism behind the shared KAN S$\rightarrow$V bias by ablating the edge parameterisation's locality while holding the rest of the architecture fixed; strengthening the prior-retention analysis from a parameter-space measurement into one linked to model outputs, for instance by testing whether perturbing a channel group's wavelets selectively degrades the class whose morphology that group is initialised for, which would let a transparency claim rest on behaviour rather than on parameters alone; retraining with a strictly causal interval vector and a causal filter to quantify what streaming compatibility costs; evaluating adaptive logit adjustment \cite{dai_angular_2026} as a beat-level alternative to RAC; and benchmarking on ARM-class hardware with a quantised wavelet-layer implementation.

%% CONCLUSION
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented PC-WavKAN, a 153,045-parameter wavelet Kolmogorov--Arnold network for inter-patient ECG beat classification whose distinguishing component is physiologically derived initialisation of the wavelet basis. Under the de Chazal DS1/DS2 protocol with 20 seeds, paired tests, multiplicity correction, effect sizes and confidence intervals, and with the architecture selected on validation data alone, it reaches a Macro-F1 of $0.357\pm0.019$, statistically indistinguishable from four standard baselines spanning 29K--118K parameters.

The contribution is therefore not accuracy. It is that an inspectable, physiologically initialised basis costs nothing in accuracy relative to standard architectures, that this structured initialisation measurably helps relative to an isotropic one --- though our design cannot yet attribute that benefit to the morphological assignment specifically --- and is substantially retained through training; and that a rarity-anchored curriculum delivers a large, reproducible gain on the class the inter-patient protocol makes hardest. The protocol also produced findings a weaker one would have missed: self-attention over a five-element interval vector is actively harmful, the mother wavelet is not consequential, both KAN bases share a supraventricular-to-ventricular bias on supraventricular-enriched data, and the rhythm features depend significantly on beats that have not yet occurred. We report these alongside the positive results because, on this evidence, they are the more transferable half of what the study established.
